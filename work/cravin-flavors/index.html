Case study · Food and beverage photography

Ninety seconds before the plate stops looking like that.

I shoot food and drink for restaurants. Plated dishes in dark dining rooms, cocktails, cafe counters, shot around a live service in a room I cannot rearrange. This is the work from five kitchens and two cafes.

01 — The shoot

One drink, five ways to look at it.

A single photo of a coffee is a nice photo. It is not a post. What performs is a carousel, so I shoot the same drink as a set: the hero angle at the table, the top-down, the macro on the foam, and at least one frame that puts the room in the shot so you can tell where you are.

All of it is available light. I move the cup to the light instead of bringing light to the cup, which is why the same drink appears on a gold table, on blue marble, and against the plant wall. Three surfaces, three moods, one order.

02 — The restaurants

Four kitchens, four different problems.

The cafe work above is one half of this. The other half is plated food in restaurants, where the constraints change completely. A drink sits still and the light is whatever the room gives you. A hot plate has about ninety seconds before the garnish wilts and the sauce skins over, and you are shooting it in a dining room you cannot rearrange, usually during service.

Jeremiah's

The widest range of the four: cocktails, a lobster roll, a steak, salads, and a plated dessert. A menu this broad is really a lighting problem, because a rosemary cocktail and a seared steak want opposite treatment and still have to look like they came from the same restaurant.

The Hallows

Dark room, dark plates, heavy comfort food. Skillet mac, buttermilk fried chicken, a queso cauldron, a margarita. Shooting dark on dark is the hardest version of this: without a hard enough light source the food turns into a brown shape, so everything here is lit to hold edges and separate the plate from the table.

Why this matters to a team

Food is the least forgiving thing to shoot on a deadline. There is no second take once it is cold, the room is not yours, and the client is running a dinner service around you. Being able to walk in, work fast, and come out with a usable set is a production skill more than a photography one, and it is the same skill that makes a shoot day on anything else go smoothly.

03 — Before and after

Fix the light. Leave the food alone.

Every frame gets graded after the shoot, and there is a hard line in how far that goes. The edit changes the lighting, the contrast, the colour, and sometimes the crop. It does not change the food.

Nothing gets added to the plate, nothing gets moved, nothing gets swapped for a better-looking version of itself. A chef plated that dish deliberately, and the placement is their design. My job is to light it and frame it so it reads the way it looked standing over it, not to rebuild it into a dish the kitchen does not actually send out. The version a customer orders has to match the picture that made them order it.

04 — The result

Shoot fast, publish on time.

Across these kitchens the job is the same: walk in, work around a live service, and come out with a set the restaurant can actually use. Menu boards, socials, delivery apps, print. One visit, one look, enough frames to cover all of it.
