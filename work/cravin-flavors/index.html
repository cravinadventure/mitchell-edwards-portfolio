Case study · Food photography

Ninety seconds before the plate stops looking like that.

Food photography is a production problem wearing a photography costume. The plate is perfect for about a minute and a half, the dining room belongs to somebody else, and the kitchen is running a service around you. This is four restaurants shot under those conditions.

01 — The clock

Everything that makes it hard has nothing to do with the camera.

A hot plate has about ninety seconds. After that the garnish wilts, the sauce skins over, the fries go soft, and the steam that made it look alive is gone. There is no second take, because the second plate costs the kitchen money and time they do not have during service.

You are also in a room you cannot change. The table is where it is, the window is where it is, and the light is whatever the room gives you at that hour. No stands, no flags, no crew, because there is a dining room full of people three feet away.

So the work happens before the plate arrives. Pick the table, read the light, know the angle, be ready. When it lands you are shooting, not deciding.

03 — The rule

Change the light. Never the plate.

Every frame gets graded after the shoot, and there is a hard line in how far that goes. The edit moves light, contrast and colour, and sometimes the crop. It does not touch the food.

Nothing gets added, nothing gets nudged into a better position, nothing gets swapped for a cleaner-looking version of itself. A chef plated that dish on purpose and the arrangement is their work, not mine to redesign.

Restaurants care about this more than photographers expect. A menu shot that oversells the plate becomes a complaint at the table two weeks later, and the kitchen is the one who wears it.

04 — Result

One visit, enough frames to cover everything.

Each restaurant got a set they could use across menu boards, socials, delivery apps and print, from a single visit that did not interrupt their service.

Why this transfers

Working fast in someone else's space, on someone else's clock, with whatever light is there, is a production skill more than a photography one. It is the same skill that makes any shoot day run clean: know the constraint before you arrive, and make the decisions in advance so the moment itself is just execution.
