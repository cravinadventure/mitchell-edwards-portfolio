01

The problem

Every creative team loses hours to the same three bottlenecks. Someone has to sit through raw footage to find the usable takes. One approved concept has to be adapted for every channel, placement and spec on the list. Finished work then has to be captioned, sized and trafficked out to each platform.

And around all of it, feedback and approvals live in three different places, so someone is always chasing a decision: is this approved, did that go out, can we get fifteen minutes to talk about it.

None of that is design work. It is the work that happens before, after and around the design, and it is where the week goes. So I build tooling that takes those jobs off people, and the hours go back into the part a client actually pays for.

02

The three places it helps

Editing. I hand off raw footage and it comes back logged and organised, with a first assembly ready to open in Premiere Pro. Large batches take real time to process, but it is machine time rather than a person's, and several shoots can run at once overnight.

Everything stays organised permanently, so footage from last year is still findable by what happens in it. Pulling clips for a new edit takes seconds instead of an afternoon of scrubbing drives, and the library gets more useful the longer it runs.

Design. I build the approved concept once and make it the source. Every adaptation the campaign needs is generated from it, so nobody rebuilds a layout by hand and nothing drifts off spec between versions.

Publishing. Finished pieces get sized to each platform, captioned and scheduled, so nothing sits done and unposted waiting on someone with a free afternoon. In a previous in-house role this same kind of setup cut scheduling time by about 75%.

03

And one layer across all three

The three above move the work forward. This part keeps everyone around it in the loop, which in my experience is where most projects actually stall.

Coordination. Decisions and deliveries get written to a shared log as they happen, so the update is already there before anyone thinks to ask. Nobody chases a status and I never stop working to write one. Check-ins land on the calendar instead of sitting in a thread for two days, and after work goes live the numbers come back in, so the next round is built on what worked instead of a guess.

The effect is fewer status meetings, because the status is written down before the meeting would have been called, and faster decisions, because the person who needs to weigh in gets asked at the moment it matters.
