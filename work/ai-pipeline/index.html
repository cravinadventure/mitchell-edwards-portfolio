How I work

Why I built them,
and how they work together

The home page lists what my agents can do. This page is about why each one exists, and how they work together with me on a real project. Every one of them started as something that went wrong, or kept going wrong, until I got tired of it and built a fix.

01

Why I built each one

The problem it fixes, in plain terms, and what it does about it.

1 · ResearchBrand breakdowns

The problem: designing for a brand you only half understand. You spend the first round guessing, and the client spends it correcting you.

Why I built it: so the guessing happens before I open a design file. Before I start, I get a full breakdown of who they are, who they are up against, and how they look, sound, and post. On Vaca Chips that read is why the bags ended up on a Newport Beach shoreline instead of a white table.

2 · DesignDesign production

The problem: one design, nine sizes. Post, story, banner, thumbnail, each one rebuilt by hand, and by the fifth one they no longer match.

Why I built it: I design the main version once and every other size gets made from it, so they all match and I stay on the part that needs a designer. TWINEM's twelve pieces each went out in three formats without me exporting thirty-six files by hand.

3 · EditingVideo editing

The problem: a folder of raw footage and a deadline. Hours go to scrubbing through clips before the actual editing starts. Months later, finding one shot means watching everything again.

Why I built it: I drop in the footage and it comes back cut into clips, each labeled with what happens in it, plus a rough first edit. Any shot stays searchable long after the project ships.

4 · MusicCut to the beat

The problem: a two-hour DJ set is a great night and a terrible piece of content. Cutting it into clips that land on the beat, and figuring out which track is playing in each one, used to eat a full day.

Why I built it: a full set comes back as short clips, cut in time with the music, with every track named and ready to post.

5 · PlanningProject timelines

The problem: next steps living in a chat thread. Everyone remembers a different version of the deadline, and something always slips.

Why I built it: deadlines, deliverables, and next steps stay mapped out in one place for every project, so I always know what comes next and so does the client.

6 · OrganizationFiles and handoffs

The problem: final_v3_FINAL_2.mov. A logo that only exists as a frame inside a video. A same-night turnaround where the right file is somewhere, and nobody is sure where.

Why I built it: every file lands in the right folder with a clear name the moment it arrives, and handoffs go out clean. On the A$AP Rocky tour visuals, a three-and-a-half-hour same-night sprint, nothing got lost.

7 · PublishingPosting and results

The problem: finished work that never posts because posting is its own job. And when something does well, nobody knows why, so it never happens again on purpose.

Why I built it: posts get sized, captioned, and scheduled. Once they are live, the numbers come back with a shot-by-shot read on what made a video work, and that goes into the next one.

8 · WebWebsite design

The problem: a portfolio that is always one project behind, because updating it means starting over every time.

Why I built it: I design the site, and the agents help build it and keep it current. New work shows up here without a rebuild. The case study pages linked on this page were written from the project record the agents kept along the way.

02

How they work together on a real project

None of these run alone. Here is what the team actually did on three projects from the work section.

LXND × A$AP Rocky TourSame-night turnaround

The brief landed at 5:44 PM and the files were in their Dropbox by 9:20 PM. Before I designed anything, the research side had already mapped the tour and produced the visual map and the proposal PDF that got the job. When the client switched the whole look from red to green mid-build, I fixed it in one move and the files and handoffs side kept every version straight, renamed every clip, sorted it into the client folder, and set up the delivery folder. The final loop and the ten-minute set-length version went out named so the media team knew which was which without asking.

TWINEM96 hours, twelve pieces

First contact Monday evening, pack complete Saturday morning. The client folder system was set up before the first asset arrived, so when the twins started sending brand files and phone footage at night, every one of them got tracked and named on the way in. I built the six loops and the custom dither tool. Design production turned twelve pieces into three formats each, plus a static logo kit. The whole way through, the agents kept the project record, and the case study page you can read now was written from that record, not from memory.

Vaca ChipsFour-day window, delivered day one

Two new flavors, packaging mockups needed before print. The brand breakdown came first: Newport Beach, cow on the logo, tallow chips, a laid-back identity already built into the label. That read is why the bags went into that world, sand and sun, instead of onto a white background. Design production turned one main direction into the full set per flavor: front face, back panel, chip bath, plus the surfboard concept they had not asked for. Everything was named, filed, and back with the client on day one of a four-day window.

03

What keeps it connected

One shared record

Every agent reads from and writes to the same project file, so no one works from old information.

Clean handoffs

When one job finishes, the next one starts with everything it needs, and the handoff gets written down.

A built-in checker

The whole system gets checked around the clock. A missed deadline or a file that never got delivered gets flagged before it becomes a problem.

It keeps learning

When I correct something, the correction becomes a rule. When a job keeps repeating, it becomes a new skill.

05

Working with your team

I don't replace what's already there, and I'm not here to replace people. I start by learning how your team and tools work, then look for the repetitive parts worth taking off their plate. Everything gets built around the software you already use.

The art stays human. Every creative decision, from the concept to the final cut, is made by a person, and the agents stay out of it. Their job is the busywork around the art, so the people on your team get more of their time back for the work only people can do.
